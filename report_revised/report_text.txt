FORECASTING KAZAKHST AN’S TOT AL MONTHL Y ELECTRICITY
GENERA TION USING SARIMA
NARLEN UALIKAN, DARIYA KUANTAY, AIBEK TUMENBAYEV, AZHAR SARBASSOVA
Abstract. This paper compares seasonal naive forecasts, a growth-adjusted seasonal naive
benchmark, and SARIMA for Kazakhstan’s national monthly electricity generation. The
frozen Ember dataset contains 102 months; the main experiment uses 84 observations
from 2019 to 2025 after a source audit. Eight low-order specifications are evaluated on
2024 forecasts; SARIMA(1,0,0)(0,1,1) 12 with a constant is retained for the 2025 test. An
unchanged-code rerun reproduces the original test MAEs: 0.435 TWh for seasonal naive
and 0.343 TWh for SARIMA. A follow-up benchmark adds the latest 12-month mean
year-on-year change, using only observations through each cutoff. Its MAE is 0.161 TWh
and RMSE is 0.217 TWh, outperforming SARIMA on this test. Thus, SARIMA’s 21.1%
MAE reduction against the original baseline does not establish an advantage over a simple
growth adjustment. There are only 12 test observations and four forecast origins. Source
construction and observed interval undercoverage limit stronger conclusions.
1.Introduction
Estimating electricity generation at the national scale provides an application of seasonal
forecasting. Short-term forecasts feed into discussions of expected production, but they do
not fully determine dispatch, consumption, or infrastructure investments. The objective
is to investigate whether SARIMA improves forecasts of monthly electricity generation
in Kazakhstan at horizons of one, two, and three months compared with repeating the
corresponding months of the previous year.
A univariate approach is taken on purpose. The comparison between a transparent seasonal
benchmark and a small classical model uses identical forecast origins and horizons. Do short-
term dependence and seasonal differencing add information beyond simply repeating last year?
A source check and sensitivity analysis help interpret the comparison. The original results
showed underprediction during a year of growth. This prompted a follow-up comparison with
a fixed growth-adjusted seasonal naive rule; it was not part of the original model selection
and is not tuned on 2025.

SARIMA(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)12 combines ordinary (p,d,q) ARIMA components with seasonal
(P,D,Q)12 components [4]. State-space maximum likelihood estimation is used in statsmodels
[5], with restrictions ensuring stationary AR and invertible MA components. For the chosen
model, define zt = yt - yt-12. Then:
zt =c+ϕz t−1 +ε t +θε t−12 (2)
The innovationsεt have zero mean and constant variance under the model. The constant
allows an average year-on-year change; it is an intercept, not directly the unconditional mean
of zt. No logarithm, external regressors, or machine-learning model is used. Eight candidates
are fixed before test scoring. The smallest unrounded 2024 validation MAE selects the order.
For an exact MAE tie, initial-training AICc breaks the tie only when all tied models share
the same differencing orders(d, D); otherwise the original candidate enumeration is retained.
There were no tied validation MAEs, so this clarification does not alter the selected model.
AICc is not the forecast accuracy criterion. All numerical fits converged.

This uses the latest 12 observed year-on-year changes, requiring 24 months of history. The

The growth-adjusted benchmark has the lowest pooled MAE (0.161 TWh) and RMSE
(0.217 TWh). Its MAE is 53.1% lower than SARIMA’s, a difference of 0.182 TWh. SARIMA
still improves on the original seasonal naive benchmark by 21.1%, but that advantage does
not persist against the simple growth adjustment. All three pooled mean errors are positive,
indicating underprediction on average; the growth adjustment reduces this bias substantially.
Every observed month in 2025 exceeds its corresponding 2024 total, which disadvantages the
original seasonal naive rule.
The horizon table contains only four forecasts per horizon. The growth benchmark has
lower MAE at horizons one and three; SARIMA has slightly lower MAE at horizon two. The
pooled ranking therefore does not imply that one method wins at every horizon or every
origin. This is a follow-up comparison motivated by the original test results, not a new
untouched confirmatory test. The improvement cannot establish statistical significance or
general superiority; another test period would be needed for an independent assessment.

The SARIMA 80% intervals cover 9 of 12 test observations (75.0%); its 95% intervals
cover 10 of 12 (83.3%). These are nominal Gaussian prediction intervals conditional on the
fitted SARIMA model and estimated parameters. They do not fully account for parameter
uncertainty or data revisions. Observed coverage falls below the nominal levels even though
point-forecast errors improve. Parameter uncertainty, model misspecification, and data
limitations may contribute, but this small test cannot establish the cause. Twelve observations
are insufficient to estimate long-run coverage precisely.
6.Sensitivity, limitations and conclusion
Including the full year 2018 in the training history, while retaining the selected SARIMA
specification, gives test MAE of 0.360 TWh and RMSE of 0.441 TWh. Seasonal naive forecasts
do not change because their corresponding months are in 2024. The growth-adjusted forecasts
also remain unchanged: at the first cutoff their adjustment uses 2023–2024 only, and later
cutoffs use more recent observations. SARIMA still beats the original seasonal naive baseline,
but remains behind the growth-adjusted benchmark (MAE 0.161 TWh). This is a sensitivity
analysis, not a step in model selection.
The analysis rests on a frozen data snapshot, not the data vintages available at each
historical forecast origin. Later data revisions or source construction may affect published
series values. Other repeated observations exist after 2018; only a small portion of the
source chain was independently verified. These forecasts should be viewed as forecasts of the
published Ember series, not independently validated measurements of physical generation.
The study is limited to a single held-out year and four forecast origins. The univariate
model does not explicitly account for weather, plant maintenance, fuel availability, trade, or
policy changes. Published monthly totals are affected by the varying number of days in each
month. Generation is not demand; historical forecasting ability does not establish a causal
relationship or an operational planning rule.

Conclusion.SARIMA improves on the original seasonal naive model in the 2025 historical
test, with a 21.1% reduction in pooled MAE. However, it does not outperform the growth-
adjusted seasonal naive benchmark: their MAEs are 0.343 and 0.161 TWh, respectively. The
more demanding comparison shows that a simple adjustment for recent annual change is
sufficient to outperform the selected SARIMA on this particular year. This benchmark was
added after seeing the original results and requires independent evaluation in future work.
Residual tests do not detect remaining autocorrelation at the tested lags, but the SARIMA
80% and 95% intervals cover only 9/12 and 10/12 test observations. A single test year, four
origins and the source audit limit stronger reliability claims. More forecast origins and a more
extensive audit of the monthly source series would strengthen the evidence.
